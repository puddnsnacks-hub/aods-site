Three years of falling roadway deaths, and what changed

After a decade of rising fatalities, the count has fallen every year since 2023. The people who work on road safety say no single program did it, and that is the point.

Published
August 20, 2026
Author
Office of Public Affairs
Reading time
6 minutes
Topics
Road safety, Data Office, Safe Streets and Roads for All

The decade that went the wrong way

Between 2011 and 2021, deaths on American roads rose from 32,479 to 42,939, the steepest ten-year climb since the 1960s. The causes were familiar and stubborn: speed, impairment, distraction, and roads designed to move cars quickly through places where people also walk and bike.

The reversal began in 2023 and has held for three consecutive years. The 2025 estimate, 37,200, is the lowest since 2019. Per mile traveled the rate is the lowest ever recorded.

What the programs did

Safe Streets and Roads for All funded 1,300 local safety plans, and the plans turned into protected crossings, narrower lanes and lower posted speeds in the corridors where the crashes were concentrated. The new vehicle rule requires automatic emergency braking on every passenger car sold after 2029, and manufacturers moved early. State highway safety offices spent more of their grant money on enforcement of impaired driving, where the evidence is strongest.

The unglamorous things

  • Road diets on 4,100 miles of urban arterials
  • Rumble strips and high-friction surfacing on rural curves
  • Lower default speed limits in 212 cities
  • Post-crash care: faster trauma transport in 31 rural counties

What comes next

The Department's target is a 30 percent reduction by 2030, measured against the 2021 peak. The 2025 count is 13 percent below it. The second half, safety officials say, is the harder half: the concentrated corridors have been addressed and what remains is spread thinly across a very large network.
