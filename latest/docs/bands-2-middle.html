Middle bands

A band is one horizontal slice of a page. A middle band is any band between the one that opens the page under the header and the one that closes it above the footer — which is to say almost all of them. It carries no page chrome of its own.

Companion pages: 1 · Initial bands covers the opening band and the header above it, and 3 · Concluding bands covers the closing band and the footer below it. The three names are the seam rule's own three cases — first, last, and everything else — so the page you are on tells you which branch of the rule applies.

What it is, and what it is for

A page is a set of bands in a useful order. Choosing the band is the first decision on a page and it settles every measurement after it: where the color reaches, where the content sits inside that color, and how far the next band stands off. Get the band right and the numbers follow from rules already written down; get it wrong and every number after it has to be argued.

A page runs header, then the band that opens under it, then however many bands carry the content, then the band that closes above the footer, then the footer. The middle bands are the ones marked — every band that has another band both above and below it. The header and the footer are chrome, not bands: they carry no ground of their own and take no part in the seam rule.

The three names are the seam rule's own three cases. First and last are the two it treats specially — 1.5 × their own line, unsplit, because each has a neighbor on one side only. Middle is every other band, and the case the rule is really about. So the page you are on tells you which branch applies.

These specimens are therefore not example pages. Each one is a single band, shown alone, with its ground, its containers and its papers named where they begin. They are the vocabulary a page is composed from — read one, then reach for it.

Vertical spacing, before anything else

Horizontal spacing in a band is fixed — 32 from the ground, then 24 from the container — and the numbers never move. Vertical spacing is derived, and it is the thing most often got wrong, so it is worth taking first.

A band declares no height. What it declares is the space at its seam with the band above, and that number is not chosen: it is 1.5 × the largest line-height in the band ABOVE, split half to each band. The band above decides, because the seam's job is to clear the type that just ended, not the type about to start.

Drawn at 1.5×, with the two bands on different grounds — which is the clearest way to see why the number is split rather than paid by one side. Each band’s 39 is drawn in its own color, so the color change lands dead center in the seam. Pay it all from one side and the boundary sits hard against one band’s type and a long way from the other’s; split it, and the edge has equal air above and below it. A display h1 at 52 hands over 78, so each ground pays 39.

Three things change that number and nothing else does:

  • A polarity change takes R = 2 rather than 1.5, so the same 52 hands over 104. Note that the two grounds drawn above are not a polarity change — both are light, so R stays 1.5. Polarity is light meeting dark, and that edge needs more air than an edge between two light colors.
  • The first and last band take 1.5 × their own line, unsplit, because there is no neighbor on that side to split with.
  • A breadcrumb band pays 16/16 and hands over 48, because a trail is not a section the user arrives at.

Every specimen further down this page is shown as the second band on a page — padded 39 top and bottom, the 78 above split. Alone on this page there is no band above to measure, so the number is pinned rather than derived; on a real page the rule computes it. ruling 65

The three components

A ground carries the color and is the section. A container carries content and no fill. A paper is a container that carries a fill and a border — always, and the border is the partner of the paper’s own fill rather than of the ground under it (ruling 17). Everything else on this page is a consequence of those three and the numbers between them.

Drawn at 1.5×, so the proportions are the page’s own. Read the bottom measure on each row: it is the same 56 either way. A container holds its content 24 in from the ground, so a component inside it begins at 56; a paper floats on its slot with a 24 margin, so the paper — which is itself the component — begins at 56 too. That is why the rule says window to first component, not to first character.

The rules so far

Everything below governs a middle band today. Where a decision has a ruling behind it, the ruling is named.

  1. Space is measured outside-inThe ground holds its content 32 in from the window; the container holds its content 24 in from the ground. Window to first component is 56 - the container's content edge, which is where a component begins. A character inside that component sits further in again, by whatever inset the component carries.
  2. Marks take no spaceAn edge, an indicator, a rail: painted, never laid out. Nothing shifts when one appears.
  3. Height is not a decision. Width is.Vertically a layout element fills what holds it: a ground, a container, a paper and a band declare no height at all - measured across the whole sheet, zero on any of them - so each takes the height of what it holds and hands its full height to what it holds. A component inside one may still hug, and the in-page nav is the case: a rail 152 tall sits in a container 543 tall that fills its ground exactly. That is the second axis's vocabulary - vertical fill for the column that spans every row, vertical hug for the component that takes only its own. Where a height IS declared it is on an object whose size is its identity - an icon, a logo, an avatar - and never on a layout element. Width, by contrast, is a real choice, and a band is largely the record of which one was made: fill, hug or set.
  4. Ink and stroke follow the fillA surface decides what may be drawn on it, not the other way round.
  5. One ground per colorA ground where the color does not change is an element with no job. Down the page, one ground per band always; across a band, one ground per color.
  6. A ground is a section, and may not be placed in a containerInside a container there is no window beside it, so the 32 a ground exists to carry would have nothing to hold against. ruling 43
  7. A paper sits in a container, never on a groundIt is a component, so the container places it - which is why it needs no margin of its own and derives no padding. Every paper is still ON a ground, because its container is; the question is only ever whether the color changed enough to need a new one.
  8. The seamR × the largest line of the band above, split 50/50 between the two bands. R = 1.5 across a ground with no polarity change; R = 2 across a ground with a polarity change. First and last band: 1.5 × its own line, unsplit. Breadcrumb band: 16/16, hands over 48. ruling 65
  9. The seam draws a divider only within a polarityTwo grounds of the same polarity meet with a 1px line; across a polarity change they meet with none, because the color change is already the edge. The line belongs to one ground and the direction of light says which: on light it is the shadowed bottom edge of the ground above, at that ground's own ramp rung +5; on dark it is the lit top edge of the ground below. It is painted, so it takes no space and the seam arithmetic above is untouched. ruling 69
  10. A paper takes the two-thirds column, never the thirdIn the narrow column a paper spends 96 of its 352 on insets and leaves 256 of text — about 29 characters, below measure-ui, a field measure rather than a reading one. In the wide column it leaves 551, the same as no paper at all. Both sides are legal, so use the other hand if the fill belongs there. ruling 70
  11. The seam is the gap between two things, not two textsTwo papers side by side hold their content 96 apart and their fills 48 apart, and the fills are what the rule is about. It is why a rail meets a container at 48 and a paper at 72 and both are correct. ruling 48
  12. A rail spans and the other column stacksA two-container ground is not limited to two: the hugging column spans every row the filling column stacks into. ruling 45
  13. A slot may span every columndata-span="full" on a container takes every column - one heading introducing both. Use it instead of nesting a ground in a container. ruling 46
  14. An in-page nav is always hug, never setThe component carries its own max-width, so the band needs only one arrangement for it: hug-left or hug-right. There is no set- variant of this pattern, so a build agent has one obvious option.
  15. The rail's two marks are mirrored about itCurrent swallows the rail and grows outward (3px, the emphasis edge). Hover starts at the rail and grows inward (2px, the emphasis border). Same axis, opposite sides, so one rail says both where you are and where you would go.
  16. The nav's outline is read off the groundA ground is a section, so the first block in a ground names it and every block after it sits inside it - h3, and one indent in the rail. The rail's shape is a picture of the band's grounds.

THE HUG-LEFT MENU BAND · every rule about grounds, in one band

div.aods-band[data-grounds="hug-left"] — four grounds, five blocks, two papers

ONE GROUND PER COLOR, read five times over. The menu is quiet, the content beside it is not, so that is a color change and there are two grounds - and the 32 goes where each ground meets the WINDOW, 0 where they meet each other, so the seam is still the two containers’ 24s with the color change dead center in it. Blocks 1 and 2 are the same color, so they are two CONTAINERS in one ground: no color change, no new ground. Block 3 is a color change, so it is a ground. EVERY PAPER IS ON A GROUND - there is no such thing as one without - so the question a paper answers is never whether it has a ground but whether it needs a NEW one, and the answer is the same rule: only if the color changed. Block 4 is a paper on ground C, the one Block 3 is already on, because nothing about that color changed. Block 5 is a paper on ground D, which exists because the color DID change - the ground is there for the color, not for the paper. AND THE OUTLINE IS A SEPARATE QUESTION FROM THE COLOR (2026-09-09). Blocks 2 and 4 are subsections because of what they SAY, not because they share a ground with the block above - a ground is a section and two peers can sit in one. They take a text heading one rung down, so the page reads as subordinate exactly where the rail indents. THE TEST IS ONE QUESTION: does the color reach the WINDOW? A ground’s does, edge to edge; a paper’s floats on its slot with a 24 margin. That is the whole difference, and it is the difference in meaning too - a ground’s block reads as a section OF the page, a paper as an object ON it. THIS IS THE ONLY SHAPE THAT WORKS beside a sticky rail: put the stack in separate bands and the rail stops being their sibling, loses the span, and drops to 48 of travel. READ THE SEAM - the rail meets every container at 48 and every paper at 72, because a paper brings a margin where a container brings only an inset.

Columns with no rail

Two arrangements, and together they are the whole two-column set a middle band needs: equal at 1:1 and third-left / third-right at 2:1. Neither has a rail — both columns are shares of the span, so nothing spans the rows and extra slots flow into rows of two. That is what separates them from the hug family above, where one column is a rail and the others stack beside it.

third names a PROPORTION, not a width, and it is named like hug and set — for the column that is not the big one. It holds 66.7 / 33.3 at every width, which is what separates it from set-right: a set column is one number that holds while the proportion drifts, measured at the aside rung from 60/40 at 1280 to 74/26 at 1920. Reach for set when the narrow column has a measure to hold; reach for third when the shape is the point.

A paper takes the two-thirds column, never the third — both sides are legal, so use the other hand if the fill belongs there. In the third a paper leaves 256 of text, about 29 characters, which is a field measure rather than a reading one; in the wide column it leaves 551, the same as no paper at all. Gated by P28.
